Dear Hiring Manager,

The gap in addiction medicine is rarely a gap in what is known. The evidence on medications for substance use disorder is established, the continuing education exists, and federal funding supports it. What determines whether a clinician actually finishes that training, claims the credit, and walks away able to use it with a patient is whether someone was there when the login failed, the certificate did not generate, or the registration page did not make sense at ten at night before a shift. ASAM's education work treats that layer as part of the mission rather than an afterthought, and closing that particular gap between what exists and what reaches someone is the work I have spent my career doing from a few different distances.

That became concrete at Casa Serena Treatment Centers, where I worked overnight and PM shifts as the only staff member on site for twenty to twenty-five women in detoxification and residential care. I was not there to explain substance use disorder in the abstract. I was there to notice when someone in withdrawal needed de-escalation instead of instruction, and to use person-first, destigmatizing language not as a policy but as the thing that kept someone willing to stay in the room. That instinct, that the words around a clinical fact matter as much as the fact itself, is the same instinct behind training healthcare professionals to talk about addiction without reinforcing the stigma that keeps people from treatment.

At Sanctuary Centers, I saw the same problem from the access side rather than the clinical side. Guiding clients through SSI, SSDI, and five concurrent housing subsidy programs, each with its own documentation and its own deadlines, taught me that most people do not get stuck on the substance of a system. They get stuck on the interface to it: a portal that assumes a kind of digital fluency they do not have, a form that asks for the wrong document first. I sat beside clients and translated that interface in real time, and I built a standardized checklist so the same translation did not have to happen from scratch for every case. Good learner support works the same way. The barrier is rarely the content of the course.

That instinct carried into national-scale education work at the National Council for Mental Wellbeing, where I moderated webinars, office hours, and community of practice calls in Zoom for CCBHC and FQHC providers, administered the LearnWorlds LMS for enrollment, progress, and certificate records, and managed continuing education compliance by reviewing attendance and awarding credit to learners. I served as the central support channel for a 3,200-member network, triaging inquiries and routing the ones I could not resolve myself. I also built a 12-module e-learning course on substance use and overdose response under HHS and SAMHSA grant requirements, which meant the plain-language, destigmatizing standard I learned at Casa Serena had to be designed into the material itself, not just into how I talked to people about it.

I want to be direct about one thing. I have not drafted continuing education applications or worked inside ACCME or Joint Accreditation requirements directly, though I have run the compliance side of CE, tracking attendance, collecting evaluations, and issuing credit. I expect there is real specificity to accreditation work that I would need to learn from ASAM's accreditation team rather than assume I already understand.

The thread from bedside to casework to national learner support has taught me the same thing at three different distances: infrastructure only does its job when someone is holding the interface steady for the person trying to use it. I would welcome the opportunity to do that for ASAM's learners. My salary expectation is within your posted range of $60,000 to $65,000.

Sincerely,

**Devika C. Dixit**
