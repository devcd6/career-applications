July 17, 2026

Dear Hiring Manager,

People's needs rarely separate as cleanly as the systems built to meet them. Housing instability and loss, availability of basic needs resources, childcare, behavioral health disorders and substance use are often overlapping or confounding factors that mutually influence one another, yet agencies remain siloed in their service and resource delivery and often only focus on a slice of an individual’s life to fit within their program’s specialization and bounds. APHSA's ACCESS initiative, aligning modernization efforts across health, human services, and labor systems so a person's full picture does not get lost between them, is the clearest example I have seen of an association building that whole-person view into its actual methodology rather than treating it as a footnote. At a time where information is cheap and systems and specialized AI solutions saturate all domains of life and service delivery, I view the early insights from the ACCESS initiative as indication of APHSA’s goal of actual community integration and delivery over just development, with the heightened focus on improving coordination networks and interagency collaboration. That is exactly the type of bridge I’ve consciously and unconsciously been working towards throughout my career, and is why I’m extremely excited to be applying for the Implementation & Innovation Associate at APHSA.

That gap became impossible to ignore during my time as Housing & Basic Needs Services Coordinator at Sanctuary Centers, where I coordinated care for a caseload of 20 to 30 adults experiencing homelessness, many with co-occurring substance use and complex trauma histories, navigating five concurrent federal and local housing subsidy programs on their behalf, each with its own documentation standards and eligibility criteria. That meant staying in constant contact with CenCal Health and county behavioral health, not because housing and health lived in separate boxes for my clients, but because the systems around them insisted they did. When a post-pandemic voucher shortage hit and demand outpaced supply overnight, I watched how quickly a person's case could stall, not because the resources did not exist, but because no single program was built to move at the speed the person's whole situation actually demanded. I built a standardized checklist system to keep every client's paperwork moving in parallel instead of one program at a time, and during that shortage it meant I could triage an entire affected caseload and secure temporary housing for every one of them without a single housing loss. Systems built in isolation from each other cost real people real time, and sometimes a roof.

At UC Santa Barbara, I saw the same problem from the research side. Coordinating recruitment across two to three concurrent labs studying family mental health communication, I learned that an interview approach that built trust with one family could feel intrusive to another, and that a survey designed around one community's assumptions could miss what mattered most to the next. Good research, like good service delivery, only holds up if it stays close enough to the ground to notice when something is not landing.

That instinct carried into national-level coordination at the National Council for Mental Wellbeing, where I have supported six or more concurrent grant-funded workstreams for a 3,200-member association. I built toolkits and implementation guides translating integrated care frameworks for Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinic partners, helping member organizations treat behavioral health as part of a person's whole health picture rather than a separate referral down the hall, ran environmental scans and state-by-state policy mapping to keep national strategy grounded in how laws and practices actually vary by state, and applied federal AI governance frameworks to evaluate new technology vendors before they reached the people those tools were meant to serve. Each piece of that work was really the same lesson as Sanctuary Centers, just at a different altitude: alignment has to be built in on purpose, or it does not happen on its own.

I want to be direct about one thing. My recent work has centered on behavioral health policy rather than the SNAP, TANF, and child welfare programs at the center of APHSA's membership, and I do not want to overstate what that experience has taught me about your members' specific day-to-day realities. What it has given me is the same discipline every role above required: translating complex, fragmented systems into something usable for the person or agency actually relying on it, and the awareness that while social determinants of health often overlap, there is so much nuance and context that there will always be parts I have still yet to learn.

I would welcome the chance to talk about how this arc, from direct service, to research, to national coordination, could contribute to the Process Innovation team's work turning what your members already know into resources that spread across state lines. My salary requirement is $76,000 to $81,000, in line with the posted range. Thank you for your time and I’d be more than happy to answer any questions you may have about my background!

Sincerely,

Devika C. Dixit
