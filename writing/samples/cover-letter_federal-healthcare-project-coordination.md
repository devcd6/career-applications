### Dear Hiring Manager,

Electronic medical records exist to make information travel faster between the people who need it. But a $30 million modernization effort doesn't succeed on the technology itself. It succeeds on whether seventy different organizations, each with their own schedules, documentation habits, and capacity constraints, can move through that transition in step with one another, and on whether the systems that come out the other side actually connect a patient's care team instead of just digitizing what used to sit in a paper chart. That coordination layer, largely invisible when it works, is where I've spent most of my career, and it's why MCD Global Health's approach to this initiative caught my attention.

I learned this first at Sanctuary Centers, coordinating housing placement for a caseload of adults navigating four separate federal and local subsidy programs at once, each with its own eligibility criteria and paperwork. No single program was complicated on its own. The complication lived in the seams between them, in the fact that nothing was designed to talk to the others. I built a centralized intake checklist so that document procurement could happen in parallel instead of in sequence, and the lesson stuck with me well past that role: systems built in isolation cost real people real time, and someone has to own the connective tissue between them.

That instinct carried into research coordination at UC Santa Barbara, where I managed IRB-compliant studies across three concurrent labs, several involving communities with a documented history of being underserved by academic research. Good methodology on paper doesn't guarantee a good outcome in practice. What worked for one participant group could feel intrusive or mismatched for the next, so protocols had to be built with enough flexibility to meet people where they were, not just where the research design assumed they'd be. That same principle applies to a rural EMR rollout spanning more than seventy organizations of varying size and technical readiness.

At the National Council for Mental Wellbeing, that instinct scaled further, and it's also where I saw the treatment-outcomes side of this problem up close. I coordinated six or more concurrent grant-funded workstreams, tracking deliverables and expenditures against 2 CFR Part 200 and SF-424A and SF-425 reporting requirements, while serving as the point of contact for twenty-five or more weekly meetings across federal partners, internal teams, and advisory groups. Part of that work involved translating Digital HEDIS and behavioral health interoperability standards, including FHIR, into language that member organizations could act on, and supporting the CCBHC-E Technical Assistance Center's health information exchange work across a national network of clinics. What that showed me is that a modernized EMR's value depends less on how much data it holds than on whether a provider can actually see a patient's full picture, medication history, referrals, prior treatment notes, at the point of care. A record that stays siloed doesn't just slow down administration. It makes coordinated, whole-person treatment harder to deliver, and that's the gap rural EMR modernization is meant to close.

I want to be direct about two things. I haven't yet worked inside an EMR modernization initiative specifically, and I don't currently have ties to Maine. What I bring instead is a track record of getting up to speed quickly inside complex, federally funded systems, and a genuine readiness to travel regularly to and within Maine as this role requires. I'd rather say that plainly than leave it for you to guess.

My work has moved through direct service, research, and national-scale coordination, and each stage taught a version of the same lesson: technology and funding can exist in abundance, but better treatment outcomes depend on whether the systems around them are built to connect, not just to store. I'd welcome the opportunity to bring that to MCD's work in Maine.

Sincerely,

**Devika C. Dixit**
