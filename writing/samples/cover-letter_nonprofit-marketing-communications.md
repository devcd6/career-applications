Dear Hiring Manager,

Global health work rarely struggles for lack of proof that it works. Community-based care, long-term partnership with local health systems, decades of outcomes data, all of it exists and consistently shows that care delivered close to where people live saves lives. What's harder to sustain is the story of that work reaching the people who fund it, especially now, as international aid contracts and organizations like Partners In Health rely more heavily on private support to keep programs running. That discipline, translating the work itself into the story that keeps it funded, is one I've built from a few different vantage points.

That need for translation became concrete at Sanctuary Centers, where I coordinated housing placement for a caseload of twenty to thirty adults navigating five separate subsidy programs, each with its own documentation standards and eligibility windows. No single client's situation fit neatly into one program's rules, and the work that mattered most wasn't the paperwork itself but making sure the right information reached the right person before a deadline passed. When a post-COVID voucher shortage hit and every program tightened at once, the caseloads that held together were the ones where communication across agencies had already been built, not improvised under pressure. The lesson stuck: information only helps the people it's meant for if someone takes responsibility for getting it to them clearly and on time.

That same instinct, adjusting the message so it lands with the audience in front of you rather than the audience you assumed, shaped how I approached participant communication in research settings at UC Santa Barbara, and it became my full-time work at the American Society of Engineers of Indian Origin, where I served as the sole communications and marketing owner for a national nonprofit. I managed six or more digital channels without dedicated support staff, kept a CMS-based website current, and built a member storytelling series from sourcing through publication that drove a 35 percent increase in membership applications and 45 percent community growth. It came from treating every channel, the newsletter, the social post, the event graphic, as one coordinated system rather than separate tasks competing for attention, not from a large team.

That discipline scaled at the National Council for Mental Wellbeing, where I partnered directly with the Marketing and Communications team to expand the visibility of program initiatives across email, web, and social channels for a 3,200-member national network. I authored a monthly newsletter reaching more than 8,000 subscribers, translating federal policy actions and clinical research into content a provider or policymaker could use, and I delivered end-to-end logistics for an 18-session national conference track, from presenter recruitment through the branded materials attendees saw on-site. At that scale, the underlying job stays the same: making sure work doesn't stay invisible to the people whose support keeps it going, just with more stakeholders and tighter timelines, with almost no room for a dropped handoff.

I want to be direct about one thing: I haven't yet worked inside a global health organization coordinating across country offices and international colleagues the way Partners In Health's Marketing and Communications team does every day. What I bring instead is a track record of learning a new system quickly and finding where its information stops flowing, then fixing that before it becomes someone else's problem. I'd expect to spend time learning how PIH's teams already communicate across sites before assuming I know how to move faster, and that's the posture I try to bring into any new environment.

The path from a single caseload to a national newsletter has taught me the same thing three different ways: the work only reaches people if someone treats getting the story out as seriously as doing the work itself. I'd welcome the opportunity to bring that instinct to Partners In Health's Marketing and Communications team.

Sincerely,
Devika C. Dixit
