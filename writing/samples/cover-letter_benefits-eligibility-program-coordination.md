July 24, 2026

Dear Hiring Manager,

Financial support systems rarely fail because the resources inside them are missing. ABLE accounts, work incentives, and asset protections all exist on paper. What's often missing is someone to walk a person through them: translating eligibility rules, tracking deadlines, and holding the pieces together across offices that each own one part of the answer. National Disability Institute's Financial Empowerment work is built around closing that gap, and it's one I've spent my career learning to close from a few different vantage points.

That understanding became concrete at Sanctuary Centers, where I coordinated care for a caseload of twenty to thirty adults experiencing housing insecurity alongside serious mental illness and complex trauma. Part of that work meant navigating SSDI, SSI, and State Disability programs alongside four separate housing subsidy systems, each with its own documentation standards and eligibility windows. No single client's needs fit inside one program's rules and every program seemed to have an unending inventory of prerequisite documentation. So I learned to build alignment by hand, one case at a time, by examining network program’s requirements individually and building a weighted matrix encompassing common documentation across them that could be commonly collected during intake. However when national vouchers and services took a hit post-Covid and qualifying standards enforcement became far stricter, I saw how quickly a fragmented system could fail someone who had already done everything asked of them, especially in regard to those navigating across systems of care. The lesson stuck: coordination determines whether services actually reach anyone, not the other way around.

That instinct carried into research at UC Santa Barbara, where I supported participant engagement across three concurrent labs, much of it with communities that standard research rarely addresses. An outreach approach that worked for one community could land as intrusive for the next, and building trust with communities commonly ignored by academia meant adjusting the method, not just the message. Good research depends on the same discipline as good coordination: noticing where a system's default assumptions don't match the person in front of you, then adjusting before that mismatch becomes another barrier.

That discipline scaled at the National Council for Mental Wellbeing, where I directed operations across six or more concurrent grant-funded workstreams, managed federal compliance reporting under 2 CFR Part 200, and coordinated with SAMHSA, CDC, and member organizations on technical assistance spanning overdose prevention and integrated care. At that scale, the fragmentation doesn't disappear. It just moves up a level: national partners, multiple funders, and dozens of moving deliverables all require the same underlying work I first learned doing intake paperwork at Sanctuary. Systems built in isolation from each other cost real people real time, whether that system is a single caseload or a national program.

I want to be direct about one thing: I haven't worked inside disability-specific benefits counseling the way NDI's team has, and I haven't navigated ABLE account mechanics or work incentive calculations firsthand. What I bring instead is a career spent learning new systems quickly and translating them for the people trying to use them, alongside direct experience in the adjacent federal and state eligibility structures that shape much of the same terrain. I'd expect to spend real time listening to NDI's Financial Empowerment team before contributing much of my own, and I think that's a mindset I often find myself adopting. As someone who has spent much time listening to individuals that support systems and resources are targeted towards,  I’ve learned that the first step to providing any amount of support is to first listen and learn.

The path from direct service to research to national coordination has taught me the same thing three different ways. The infrastructure for financial security already exists. What decides whether people with disabilities actually benefit from it is whether someone is doing the quiet, deadline-driven work of holding the pieces together. I'd welcome the opportunity to bring that work to NDI's Financial Empowerment division.

Sincerely,

**Devika C. Dixit**
