Dear Hiring Manager,

In a time when information is cheap and data is everywhere, a lack of evidence rarely explains why care doesn't reach someone. The gap is usually a structural lack of cognizance: systems that were never built to notice how needs and barriers shift from one community to the next, or to listen closely enough to fold what they learn back into solutions built with a community, not just implemented on it. Sostento's model, building solutions alongside the frontline health workers who will use them rather than handing solutions down from a distance, is the first organization I've seen build that lesson into its actual process instead of treating it as an afterthought.

That kind of awareness became concrete on the ground. As Housing & Basic Needs Services Coordinator at Sanctuary Centers, I coordinated care for a caseload of 20 to 30 adults experiencing homelessness, many with co-occurring substance use and complex trauma histories, helping them navigate federal, state, and local care systems, each with its own documentation standards and eligibility criteria. That work showed me the human cost of solution-building done at a distance, no matter how well the research behind it held up. In the post-pandemic landscape, when services were scarce and demand was high, the systems meant to deliver resources quickly to the people who needed them most became one of the biggest gaps instead. One clear example was digital access: the barrier wasn't just physical, plenty of people didn't own a device, it was also a knowledge gap, since documentation portals and voucher applications assumed a baseline of technology literacy that not everyone had. I ended up sitting beside people to fill out forms myself, translating jargon-heavy instructions into plain language as we went. The lesson stuck: a solution only works if it's designed for the person actually using it, not just the policy it's meant to satisfy.

At UC Santa Barbara, I saw the same problem from the research side. Coordinating two concurrent public health communication labs, I managed participant recruitment and interview facilitation on sensitive topics like family mental health and racial stigma, and synthesized qualitative and quantitative findings into presentations and briefs for principal investigators. Recruiting across real demographic and cultural range taught me that an approach that felt natural to one participant could feel intrusive to another. Good research and assessment work means building in room to adjust, not assuming one approach travels evenly.

That instinct carried into national-level coordination at the National Council for Mental Wellbeing, where I supported a distributed team across six or more concurrent grant-funded workstreams. I developed implementation toolkits and health education materials for a national network of Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinic partners, tracked clinic and stakeholder engagement through Asana and shared trackers, and managed compliance documentation that fed directly into leadership and funder-facing reporting. I also handled meeting logistics and same-day notes for senior program advisors, and produced slide decks and briefs that translated technical research into materials a policymaker or clinic partner could use. From casework to national coordination, the lesson held up every time: the work only holds up if it stays close enough to the ground to notice when something isn't landing.

I'd welcome the opportunity to bring that same combination, structured coordination and steady attention to the partners on the ground, to Sostento's work on this project. I'm based in Texas, one of the states where Sostento is currently registered to employ staff, and I'm available to start as soon as needed. Thank you for your time and consideration, and for the work Sostento does to make sure no one falls through the cracks of our healthcare system.

Sincerely,
