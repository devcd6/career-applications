Dear Hiring Manager,

Resources for mental health rarely fail to exist. NAMI has already built the peer-led and family-led programs, the research partnerships, and the national reach that most organizations in mental health advocacy spend decades trying to assemble. What usually determines whether any of that reaches a person in crisis is not the quality of the program design, it is whether the operational structure underneath it, the calendars, the deliverable timelines, the grant compliance, the flow of information between a Chief Program Officer and the National Directors executing the work, actually holds together well enough for the program to move from design to delivery. That is the layer I have spent my career working inside, and it is why I am applying for the Senior Administrative Coordinator, Programs & Services position.

That lesson became concrete on the ground at Sanctuary Centers, where I coordinated housing and basic needs services for a caseload of 20 to 30 adults with serious mental illness and co-occurring substance use disorders, navigating five concurrent federal, state, and local subsidy programs on their behalf, each with its own documentation standards and eligibility criteria. When a post-pandemic voucher shortage hit and demand outpaced supply overnight, the resources people needed had not disappeared, the systems built to deliver them simply were not built to move at the speed a real crisis demanded. I designed a standardized checklist system so every client's paperwork could move in parallel instead of one program at a time, and it meant I could triage an entire affected caseload and secure temporary housing without a single housing loss. Coordination, not the existence of resources, was the variable that decided outcomes.

At UC Santa Barbara, I saw a version of the same problem from the administrative side of research. Coordinating operations across three concurrent communication labs meant maintaining separate tracking systems, timelines, and stakeholder expectations for each principal investigator, while making sure none of that complexity became visible to the people relying on the work getting done. Good coordination, I learned, is mostly invisible when it is working. It only becomes visible when it fails, and by then something has usually already slipped.

That instinct carried directly into national-level coordination at the National Council for Mental Wellbeing, where I supported a Chief Program Officer equivalent leadership function across six or more concurrent grant-funded workstreams for a 3,200-member behavioral health association. I owned the executive calendar and scheduling across 20 to 25 weekly touchpoints spanning federal partners, clinicians, and internal leadership, and acted as the first line of triage for competing priorities, escalating only what warranted leadership's attention rather than everything that landed in an inbox. I built the centralized tracking infrastructure in Asana and Airtable that let us hit 100 percent on-time deliverable completion across evolving federal funder timelines, translated technical interoperability discussions with organizations like NCQA and CARF into decision-ready materials, and reconciled vendor expenditures against federal grant budgets to produce audit-ready compliance documentation. Each piece of that work was really the same lesson as Sanctuary Centers, just at a different altitude. Alignment between a leader's priorities and an organization's daily operations has to be built on purpose, or it quietly stops happening.

I want to be direct about one thing. My national-level coordination experience, while dense in scope, has been concentrated in a single role and a single organization's internal program office rather than a federated network like NAMI's, where a Chief Program Officer's priorities have to translate not just across internal departments but across hundreds of independently governed State Organizations and Affiliates. I do not want to overstate how long I have held that exact function. What that role did give me was the discipline every layer above required, protecting a leader's time and attention well enough that the work under them could keep moving, and the awareness that in a structure as large and federated as NAMI's Alliance, there will be context about how that coordination actually plays out in the field that I have yet to learn.

I would welcome the opportunity to talk about how this arc, from direct service, to research administration, to national-level executive support, could contribute to the Programs & Services team's work keeping initiatives like NAMI Family-to-Family and Peer-to-Peer moving from the CPO's priorities into national execution. My salary requirement is in line with the posted range of $52,650 to $58,500. Thank you for your time and consideration, and for the work NAMI does to make sure education, support, and advocacy actually reach the people who need them.

Sincerely,

Devika C. Dixit
